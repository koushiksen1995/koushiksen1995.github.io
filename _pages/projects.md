Algol binary stars

The term "Algol" refers to its prototype Beta Persei, a bright multiple star system in the constellation Perseus. It is one of the first stars recorded to vary in brightness, dating back to 1244–1163 BC.

<details> <summary>Read more</summary>

For Batman enthusiasts, the word "Algol" derives from the Arabic Ra's al-Ghul, the head of the demon. For a fuller historical background, see the Wikipedia page.

These systems have short orbital periods (a few days) and are, more often than not, observable as double-lined eclipsing binaries. They are therefore ideal for studying stellar and binary properties, since precise measurements of component masses, radii and effective temperatures are possible. They also constitute a long-lived phase of close binary evolution in which we observe a unique configuration: the less massive star transfers mass to its more massive companion. More than 50 massive Algol binaries have been observed across the Milky Way, the Large Magellanic Cloud and the Small Magellanic Cloud combined.

These properties make Algol binaries a unique test bed for theories of massive stellar and binary evolution, and in particular for the physics of binary interaction directly. I study the evolution of massive Algol binaries — those heavier than eight times the mass of the Sun — using detailed binary evolution models computed with MESA (Modules for Experiments in Stellar Astrophysics). By comparing the model predictions against the observed properties of massive Algols, I derive constraints on binary and stellar physics: mass-transfer efficiency, internal mixing, stellar winds, and so on.

Wolf-Rayet stars

Wolf-Rayet stars are the brightest and hottest stars observable in the sky. Their spectra show broad emission lines of hydrogen, helium, nitrogen, oxygen and carbon, and they drive strong radiation-driven winds.

<details> <summary>Read more</summary>

Those winds enrich the interstellar medium with heavy elements. While most Wolf-Rayet stars are expected to be burning helium in their cores, a handful sit on the main sequence of the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram and are presumably burning hydrogen in their cores instead. These stars are thought to be the direct progenitors of black holes and neutron stars, so understanding their formation and evolution refines our knowledge of gravitational-wave astronomy directly.

I have shown that the evolution of very massive binaries challenges the conventional picture of binary evolution, all of it traceable to these stars having luminosities close to the Eddington limit. Observed counterparts confirm the existence of a novel channel in which a more massive star transfers mass to a less massive companion on the nuclear timescale — reverse Algols. The mass donors can be observable as Wolf-Rayet stars, with BAT99 113 the smoking gun. I am studying the observable properties of this class of binaries to understand their impact on black hole formation and, in turn, on the strongest sources of gravitational-wave emission.

Gravitational-wave progenitors

The discovery of gravitational waves has given us a new pair of glasses through which to observe the Universe. With roughly 100 detections by LIGO, and detectors such as LISA, Taiji and the Einstein Telescope expected in the coming decades, there are exciting years ahead.

<details> <summary>Read more</summary>

Accurate merger-rate predictions remain difficult. Most rapid binary population synthesis codes used to predict the properties of gravitational-wave merger events rely on simple single-star models. Significant uncertainties also arise from the tension in our understanding of the progenitor systems described above.

With the rise in available computing power, calculating extensive grids of massive single and binary stellar evolution models has become possible. I am building grids of state-of-the-art models at several metallicities to feed into the rapid binary evolution code COMPAS, in order to derive more precise gravitational-wave merger rates. A better understanding of massive star evolution from the projects above, implemented in the detailed grids, further improves that accuracy. The same models can be applied to many other systems: the evolution of globular clusters, gamma-ray bursts and core-collapse supernovae.
